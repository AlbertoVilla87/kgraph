Imagine deploying a reasoning model that scores 92% on an offline benchmark. During evaluation, its answers are polished, its derivations appear rigorous, and its chain of thought resembles the work of a careful analyst. In production, however, the model begins to exploit formatting artifacts in the input, repeat unnecessary verification loops, call expensive tools when simple arithmetic would suffice, and occasionally produce a correct answer after an invalid intermediate step. The benchmark says the model can reason. The operational evidence says something more complicated: the model has learned a policy that sometimes produces useful reasoning-shaped text. This gap between apparent reasoning and dependable problem-solving is the central challenge addressed by Chain-of-Thought Reinforcement Learning, or CoT RL.

Chain-of-thought prompting was originally shown to elicit intermediate steps, substantially improving performance on arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning tasks. The important operational insight was not merely that models benefit from producing more tokens, but that additional computation expressed through intermediate tokens can create a trajectory through the problem rather than forcing the model to jump directly from prompt to answer. CoT RL extends this idea by treating the generated trajectory as the behavior of a policy that can be sampled, evaluated, and optimized. Instead of asking only, "Can the model imitate a high-quality solution?", practitioners ask, "Which reasoning behaviors should become more probable after repeated interaction with a reward signal?"

That distinction changes everything. Supervised fine-tuning trains a model to reproduce demonstrations. Reinforcement learning trains it to allocate probability mass toward trajectories that earn higher rewards. A CoT-trained model may learn the surface form of decomposition, verification, backtracking, or self-correction from examples. A CoT RL model can, at least in principle, discover when those behaviors improve task success, when they are unnecessary, and when a different reasoning strategy should be attempted. The target is no longer a canonical explanation. The target is a policy over possible reasoning trajectories [1-5].
